## Vertical Context Pre-Load

**IMPORTANT:** All objection responses in this skill are calibrated for selling TO home service business owners — HVAC companies, plumbers, roofers, electricians, pest control operators, landscapers, and related trades. These are NOT corporate buyers. They are owner-operators who:

- Make decisions alone or with one other person (spouse, partner)
- Are skeptical of salespeople by default (they've been burned before)
- Respond to peer proof and specific numbers, not features and frameworks
- Have 8 minutes of attention before they tune out
- Use plain language — not business school vocabulary
- Run on cash flow, not P&L projections
- Care about their Google rating the way most people care about their credit score

**Every response in this playbook is written in language that a home service business owner will actually hear and respond to.**

## Objection Handling Frameworks

Every response uses one of two frameworks. Both versions are provided for each objection.

### Framework 1: Feel-Felt-Found (FFR)
```
"I hear you — [restate their concern in their language].
[Another contractor in their trade] felt the same way.
What they found was [specific outcome with real numbers from home services context]."
```

### Framework 2: Acknowledge-Bridge-Close (ABC)
```
Acknowledge: [Validate genuinely — never dismiss]
Bridge: [Reframe with home services-specific context]
Close: [Specific proof point + a next step that takes under 10 minutes of their time]
```

## The 15 Universal Objections — Home Services Edition

### Objection 1: "It's too expensive" / "I can't afford it"

**What it really means in home services:** Cash flow is tight (common — especially in shoulder season), or they don't yet see the ROI clearly enough. Very rarely means they literally cannot afford it — a company running 5 trucks spends more on fuel every month.

**FFR Response:**
"I hear you — money's tight and you're careful about what you spend it on. That's exactly the right instinct. A roofing company in [city] felt the same way when we first talked. He was spending $1,800/month on Angi and closing maybe 1 in 5 of those leads. What he found was that after 60 days with us, his Google rating went from 4.1 to 4.6 and he was getting 11 more inbound calls per month — calls he owned, not rented from Angi. He cut his Angi spend in half and came out ahead. I'm not asking you to add a cost — I'm asking you to shift one."

**ABC Response:**
"Totally fair — and I'm not going to try to talk you into something that doesn't make financial sense. (Acknowledge) Here's the thing though: what are you spending on Angi or Google Ads right now per month? (Bridge — let them answer) OK — so you're spending [X] to rent leads that go to 3 other contractors too. What I do is help you own leads that only come to you. If I can show you that the math works in your favor, would you be open to looking at the numbers together? Takes 10 minutes. (Close)"

**Follow-up question:** "Let me ask it this way — what are you spending per month on lead generation right now, between Angi, Google Ads, or whatever you're using? I want to show you where this fits relative to what you're already spending."

**Proof point:** "Jake's HVAC in [city] — 7 trucks, just like you — was paying $2,200/month between Angi and Google Ads. After 90 days, he'd cut Angi spend to $800 and replaced it with 14 more Google calls per month. Net result: he's spending less and getting more jobs. That's the math I want to show you."

**When to walk away:** If they are genuinely in financial distress (just lost a major account, slow season cash crunch with no line of credit, or recently laid off techs), offer to reconnect after their next busy season. Don't push into debt — they'll churn and resent you.

### Objection 2: "We get all our work from referrals — we don't need this"

**What it really means in home services:** They are proud of their reputation (a good sign — means they do quality work) but are dangerously dependent on a channel they cannot control or scale. This is one of the most common and most solvable objections in home services.

**FFR Response:**
"That's actually the best thing I could hear — means your customers love you. Dave who runs a plumbing company in [city] said the same thing. Built his whole business on referrals, 15 years, never needed marketing. What he found was that when his best referral customer moved out of state, he had a rough 6 months. Referrals are great — you just can't turn them up when you need more trucks rolling. What happens for you in January when referrals slow down?"

**ABC Response:**
"That's honestly a sign you're doing great work — not every contractor can say that. (Acknowledge) The only problem with referrals is you can't control them — you can't decide to get 10 more this month because you added a truck. What if you could turn that same great reputation into a Google presence that sends you calls 24/7, without waiting for a neighbor to mention your name? (Bridge) Real quick — what's your Google rating sitting at right now? (Close — their answer opens the conversation)"

**Follow-up question:** "How many Google reviews do you have right now? Because most of the referral-based businesses I talk to have great work and terrible online presence — and you're leaving calls on the table every week because of it."

**Proof point:** "Maria's Cleaning Service in [city] — 100% referral business for 8 years — had 14 Google reviews and a 4.2 rating. We got her to 4.8 with 110 reviews in 4 months. She added 9 new recurring clients that month alone, none of them referrals — just Google searches. Referrals are still her #1 source, but now Google is #2."

**When to walk away:** If they are literally at capacity and have no interest in growth, they genuinely don't need more leads. Add to nurture for when they add a truck or hire more staff.

### Objection 3: "I don't have time for this"

**What it really means in home services:** This is the most common and most legitimate home services objection. They are dispatching, quoting, answering phones, and managing techs — all simultaneously. It is also often a shield for "I don't want to be sold to."

**FFR Response:**
"I know — you're running the show and every minute counts. Tony who runs an electrical company in [city] said those exact words to me. He was handling dispatch, quotes, and customer calls all at once. What he found was that he was wasting 3 hours a week following up manually for reviews that never came in. We automated that completely — now he gets 6-8 new reviews a month on autopilot and hasn't thought about it since. I'm not asking for your time — I'm trying to give some back."

**ABC Response:**
"I completely respect that — the last thing you need is another meeting. (Acknowledge) Here's what I'd ask: give me 8 minutes right now, or pick a Tuesday or Thursday before 9am when things are quiet. I'm not going to demo you a product for 45 minutes — I'm going to show you one thing about your Google profile that's costing you calls right now, and if you don't think it's worth fixing, hang up. (Bridge) What's better — Tuesday at 7:30 or Thursday? (Close)"

**Follow-up question:** "When's a quiet 8 minutes for you? I'm not asking for an hour — I'm asking for less time than it takes to drink a cup of coffee."

**Proof point:** "Everything I set up takes less than 2 hours of your team's time total. After that it runs automatically. I'm not adding to your workload — I'm removing something from it."

**When to walk away:** If they truly cannot find 8 minutes in the next 2 weeks, add to a 30-day callback sequence. Don't push — they will associate your brand with pressure and stop picking up.

### Objection 4: "Send me more information"

**What it really means in home services:** 95% of the time this is the polite brush-off. Home service owners don't read brochures — they call other contractors they trust and ask for recommendations.

**FFR Response:**
"Happy to send something. Chris who runs a pest control company said the same thing. I sent him a 3-page PDF and he never read it. What actually worked was a 10-minute call where I showed him his own Google profile compared to his top competitor in the same zip code. Seeing it side-by-side was what made it click for him. Can I do that with you instead of sending a generic document?"

**ABC Response:**
"Absolutely — I'll send something over. (Acknowledge) Before I do, let me ask — what would actually help you make a decision? Because most guys I talk to in your position have 30 PDFs in their inbox they'll never open. (Bridge) What if instead I sent you a one-page before/after from an HVAC company in [their state] with the same truck count as you? That plus a 10-minute call where I show you your specific Google profile — would that be more useful? (Close)"

**Follow-up question:** "What specifically would help you decide — is it pricing, is it seeing results from someone like you, or something else? I want to send the right thing, not just a brochure."

**Proof point:** Offer to send a single case study from a contractor in their specific trade and region — not a generic company overview.

**When to walk away:** If they insist on email-only and don't respond to 2 follow-up touches, move to a 90-day nurture sequence. Some owners need to see your name 4-5 times before they'll take a call.

### Objection 5: "We're not ready right now" / "Maybe next quarter"

**What it really means in home services:** Usually one of three things: (1) it's peak season and they're buried, (2) it's dead slow season and they have no cash, or (3) soft no because they don't see urgency.

**FFR Response:**
"That makes sense — timing matters a lot in this business. An HVAC company owner in [city] said the same thing in July — he was slammed with AC calls and couldn't think about anything else. What he found was that by the time October came around, he was set up and ready, and his slow season was 40% busier than the previous year because his Google rating had climbed from 3.9 to 4.7. The best time to work on your off-season is during your busy season. When does yours usually slow down?"

**ABC Response:**
"Totally understand — there's a season for everything in this business. (Acknowledge) Here's the thing though: if I set this up during your busy season, it's working for you by the time your slow season starts. You don't have to do anything during peak — I handle the setup. (Bridge) When does your busy season typically wind down? I want to time this so you're getting calls during the months you need them most. (Close)"

**Follow-up question:** "Is the timing more about bandwidth right now, or more about budget? Those are different problems — if it's bandwidth, I can set everything up with less than 2 hours of your time and you won't notice. If it's budget, let's look at timing that around your busy season when cash is better."

**When to walk away:** Get a specific month and call back then. Set a calendar reminder for 2 weeks before that date. Do not let "next quarter" become a black hole.

### Objection 6: "I need to talk to my wife / partner / accountant"

**What it really means in home services:** Completely legitimate in family businesses (which most home service companies are). The spouse often handles finances or has veto power. Respect this completely — pushing against it will kill the deal.

**FFR Response:**
"Absolutely — this should be a family decision. Mike who runs a plumbing company out here had the same process. His wife handles the books and had to sign off. What I did was put together a simple one-page comparison — what they were spending on Angi vs. what I cost vs. what the expected return looked like in 90 days. She approved it the same week. Want me to put that together for you to share with her?"

**ABC Response:**
"That's exactly the right call — especially if she handles the finances. (Acknowledge) I want to make that conversation as easy as possible for you. I know how hard it is to walk in and say 'I want to try this new thing' without the right information. (Bridge) Let me put together a one-page ROI summary — your current lead gen cost, what this costs, and what the realistic return looks like in 90 days. Then if you want, I'm happy to be on a call with both of you to answer her questions directly. Would that help? (Close)"

**Follow-up question:** "What do you think her main concern will be — is it the monthly cost, or making sure there's a clear return? I'll make sure I address that specifically."

**When to walk away:** If after 3 weeks there's been no decision and they can't arrange a joint call, send a breakup email — "I don't want to keep taking up your time if the timing isn't right. I'll reach back out in [season]. If anything changes before then, here's my cell."

### Objection 7: "We tried [review software / marketing agency / Angi] before and it didn't work"

**What it really means in home services:** Real trauma. Home service owners get sold to constantly — by Angi reps, by marketing agencies, by software companies. Many have been burned. This objection requires genuine empathy, not a sales pivot.

**FFR Response:**
"I don't blame you one bit — this industry has a lot of people making promises they can't keep. What specifically went wrong? Because if it's the same thing I do, I'd rather tell you right now and not waste your time. I've talked to guys who paid an agency $3,000 a month and got nothing, and I've talked to guys who tried review software that nobody on the team actually used. Those are different problems. What happened with yours?"

**ABC Response:**
"That's really important and I'm glad you told me. (Acknowledge) The worst thing I could do is put you through another bad experience. Can I ask what specifically failed? Was it the product itself, was it the setup process, or did the team just never use it? (Bridge) I ask because most of the failed implementations I've seen in home services come down to one of two things — either the product wasn't built for field service workflows, or there was nobody to do the setup. I want to tell you honestly whether we'd be different, and I can only do that if I know what broke. (Close)"

**Follow-up question:** "What did you try, and what specifically didn't work? I'm not asking so I can tell you 'we're different' — I want to actually know if we'd have the same problem."

**Proof point:** If you have a customer who also had a previous bad experience with the same type of product and succeeded with yours, lead with that story specifically.

**When to walk away:** If the previous failure was recent (last 6 months), deeply painful, or cost them significant money, the wound is too fresh. Plant a seed and come back in 6-12 months when the memory has faded and the pain is still real.

### Objection 8: "Your competitor offers X that you don't"

**What it really means in home services:** They've done some research (rare — good sign!) or a competitor rep got to them first. Find out if the missing feature actually matters to their specific workflow.

**FFR Response:**
"You're right — [Competitor] does have [Feature X], and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. A plumbing company owner I work with raised the same point. What he found was that [Feature X] looked important on paper but he never actually used it. What mattered for his business was [your differentiator] — specifically the [Jobber/HCP] integration that pulls job completion automatically so his office manager doesn't have to do anything manual. Does [Feature X] come up in how your team actually works day-to-day?"

**ABC Response:**
"You're right — they have that and we don't, and I appreciate you being direct about it. (Acknowledge) Help me understand — how often would you actually use [Feature X] in your workflow? I want to figure out if that's a dealbreaker or a nice-to-have before we go further. (Bridge) The reason I ask is that a lot of the guys who choose [Competitor] over us end up coming back because [Feature X] didn't matter as much as they thought, and what did matter was [your key differentiator]. I'd rather you know that now. (Close)"

**When to walk away:** If it's a genuine hard requirement — e.g., they need a specific integration that only the competitor has — be honest. "If [Feature X] is a true requirement for your workflow, [Competitor] is probably the right call for now. I'd love to stay in touch because we're building toward that." Honesty in home services is extraordinarily rare coming from salespeople. It builds long-term trust.

### Objection 9: "I can build this myself / I can just ask customers for reviews myself"

**What it really means in home services:** They believe manual outreach works — which it does, just not consistently. They're underestimating the drop-off in execution once the business gets busy.

**FFR Response:**
"You absolutely can — and honestly some guys do. The HVAC owner across town from you said the same thing and gave it a shot for 3 months. He asked customers verbally at the end of every job. He got 4 reviews. What he found was that the week he got slammed with calls, reviews stopped completely — not because he didn't care, but because he had 14 other things to handle. Automating it meant it kept working even during his busiest weeks. He went from 4 reviews in 3 months manually to 22 reviews in 6 weeks automated."

**ABC Response:**
"You definitely can ask manually — and if it's working great, you don't need me. (Acknowledge) Here's the question: how many reviews have you gotten in the last 60 days? And how consistent has it been week to week? (Bridge — let them answer) That's what I thought. Most guys who ask manually get 1-2 a month when it's quiet, then zero during busy season when they're running 15 jobs a day. The point of automating it isn't to do something you can't do — it's to make sure it never stops even when life gets hectic. (Close)"

**Follow-up question:** "How many Google reviews have you gotten in the last 90 days? And how many jobs have you completed? I want to show you the conversion rate so you can decide if the manual approach is working."

**When to walk away:** If they are genuinely doing it well manually (20+ reviews in last 3 months, consistent), they may not need automation. Pivot to what else you offer that they don't have covered.

### Objection 10: "I don't see the ROI"

**What it really means in home services:** The connection between reviews/marketing/your product and actual jobs booked hasn't been made concrete enough. Home service owners think in jobs and revenue, not in abstract metrics.

**FFR Response:**
"Fair — if I can't show you the ROI in real numbers, you shouldn't buy it. An electrician in [city] said the same thing. I showed him this: he had a 3.9 rating on Google and 28 reviews. His top competitor had a 4.7 and 190 reviews. In their zip code, the 4.7 guy was getting 3x the Google calls. He was at capacity — which meant he was getting 3x the calls with the same number of trucks. That gap was costing him roughly $180K in revenue he could have had. Does that math land for you?"

**ABC Response:**
"If you can't see the ROI, I haven't explained it right — that's on me. (Acknowledge) Let me try a different angle: what's your average job ticket? (Let them answer.) OK, so $350. And how many calls do you get per month from Google right now? (Let them answer.) Here's what I've seen in companies like yours — every full star rating point on Google adds roughly 8-12% more calls. You're at a [X] rating — if we get you to 4.7, that's [X]% more calls, which at your average ticket and close rate is [$ amount] per month. Want me to run that math for your numbers specifically? (Close)"

**Proof point:** "The ROI math for review management in home services is simple: more 5-star reviews = higher Google Maps ranking = more inbound calls = more jobs. A 1-point rating improvement for a 5-truck HVAC company translates to roughly $12,000-18,000 in additional annual revenue at average conversion rates."

**When to walk away:** If after doing the math for their specific situation the numbers genuinely don't add up (very small market, already at 4.9 with 300 reviews, pricing not competitive), tell them. You'll earn more trust walking away honestly than pushing a bad deal.

### Objection 11: "We're locked into a contract with [Angi / another vendor]"

**What it really means in home services:** Real constraint. Angi contracts are 12 months. Agency contracts are often 6-month minimums. Respect this — but use it to start the conversation about what comes next.

**FFR Response:**
"Totally understand — breaking a contract early isn't worth it. When does it expire? Because what I want to do is start building your owned presence now so that when the contract ends, you're not starting from zero. I had a roofing company go through the same thing — waited out their Angi contract, and we had 4 months of setup time so by the time they cancelled, their Google calls had already replaced what they'd been getting from Angi."

**ABC Response:**
"Smart — don't break a contract early if you don't have to. (Acknowledge) Here's what I'd suggest: let's figure out what life looks like after that contract ends so you're not scrambling. When does it expire? (Bridge) I can start the review and Google presence work now — it takes 3-4 months to see meaningful results anyway. By the time your contract ends, you'll have something to replace it with instead of scrambling. (Close)"

**Follow-up question:** "When does your current contract end? And when it does, what's your plan — do you renew, or are you looking for something different?"

**When to walk away:** This is a timing issue, not a rejection. Get the contract end date, set a reminder 8 weeks before that date, and start nurturing. The pain of Angi is a gift that keeps giving — they will be ready to talk.

### Objection 12: "This isn't a priority right now"

**What it really means in home services:** Other fires are burning. Could be a new truck, a key tech who just quit, a bad job gone legal, or just peak season. Need to identify the real reason before deciding whether to wait or reframe.

**FFR Response:**
"I hear you — there's always something. What's on your plate right now? Because half the time when I ask that question, I find out it's something that my work actually helps with. Like the time I talked to a plumber who said the same thing — and it turned out the 'not a priority' was because he'd just lost his best tech and was underwater. When I asked what he needed most, it was more inbound calls so he didn't need to keep that tech. That's exactly what I work on."

**ABC Response:**
"Totally fair — you can't fix everything at once and something has to wait. (Acknowledge) Can I ask what IS the top priority right now? (Bridge — let them answer.) OK — and does [their answer] get harder when you have fewer calls coming in? Because sometimes the thing that seems like the priority is actually made worse by not having enough work in the pipeline. (Close)"

**When to walk away:** Get a specific timeframe. "When do you think this would move up the list?" If they can't name a month, they're telling you it's never. Add to nurture and check back in one quarter.

### Objection 13: "We don't have bandwidth to implement"

**What it really means in home services:** They've been burned by complicated software implementations before. "Implementation" sounds like months of work. Reframe what implementation actually looks like for your product.

**FFR Response:**
"Implementation sounds like a lot — I know. An HVAC company owner told me the same thing. He thought it would take weeks to set up. What he found was that our entire setup took one 45-minute call with his office manager, and after that she didn't have to do anything. The system connects to their job completion workflow and sends review requests automatically. His office manager said it was easier than setting up a new email account."

**ABC Response:**
"That's a real concern and I don't want to add work to your team's plate. (Acknowledge) Here's what implementation actually looks like: one 45-minute call with your office manager, we connect it to [Jobber/HCP/whatever FSM they use], and it runs on its own after that. Your team's total ongoing time investment is zero — it just runs. (Bridge) I can walk you through what that call looks like in 5 minutes right now so you can see exactly what's involved. Want to see it? (Close)"

**Follow-up question:** "What's your biggest concern about implementation specifically — is it the initial setup time, the ongoing management, or getting your techs to change how they do something?"

### Objection 14: "How do I know this will work for us?"

**What it really means in home services:** They want peer proof. Not case studies from a dental practice. Not tech company logos. They want to hear it from another contractor in their trade, in a market like theirs.

**FFR Response:**
"That's exactly the right question — and I'd be concerned if you just took my word for it. Here's what I'd offer: I work with an HVAC company in [neighboring city] with [similar truck count]. I can connect you directly with the owner. 10-minute phone call, owner to owner — he can tell you what worked, what didn't, and what he'd do differently. That's more valuable than anything I could show you in a demo. Want me to make the intro?"

**ABC Response:**
"You should absolutely have proof before spending money on anything. (Acknowledge) The most useful proof I can give you isn't a case study — it's a direct conversation with another contractor in your trade who's already doing this. (Bridge) I have [trade] company owners in [region] I can connect you with. 10-minute call, no pressure, they'll tell you the truth. Would that help you make the decision? (Close)"

**Follow-up question:** "What would 'working for you' specifically look like? More Google calls? Higher rating? Fewer hours chasing reviews? If you tell me the metric that matters most, I can show you exactly how we've moved that number for someone like you."

**When to walk away:** If you don't have relevant case studies in their trade and geography, be honest. "I'm going to be straight with you — my best reference customers are in [trade], not [their trade]. I think the approach works the same way but I can't point you to someone exactly like you. If that's a dealbreaker, I understand."

### Objection 15: "Just not interested"

**What it really means in home services:** Could be a genuine no, could be they're busy, could be they've been called by 3 other people this week selling the same thing and you sound like all of them.

**FFR Response:**
"Fair enough — I appreciate the straight answer. Before I let you go, one quick question: most [trade] owners I talk to tell me either reviews or lead gen cost is the one thing they'd fix if they could. Is either of those on your radar, or have you got that handled? If you've got it sorted, I genuinely don't need to call you again. If one of those is a headache, I might be worth 8 minutes."

**ABC Response:**
"I hear you — and I'm not going to push. (Acknowledge) Can I ask one thing before I go? Was it how I framed this, or is the problem space just not relevant for you? (Bridge) Because if I described it wrong, I'd want to fix that. And if you've genuinely solved the review and lead gen problem, I want to know how — I'd learn something. (Close)"

**Follow-up question:** "Last question: what's your Google rating sitting at? If it's 4.8 with 200+ reviews, you don't need me. If it's not, I might be worth one conversation."

**When to walk away:** One genuine attempt. If they say no again, say "Understood — I'll stay out of your hair. If anything changes with your Google presence or Angi spend, here's my cell." Then stop calling.

### Objection 17: "My nephew / my buddy handles my marketing"

**What it really means:** A personal relationship is protecting an incumbent vendor. Common in family businesses and tight-knit trade communities. Don't attack the relationship — route around it.

**FFR Response:**
"I respect that — and I'm not trying to step on that relationship or take work away from someone you trust. An electrician I work with had the same situation — his cousin built his website and handled his Facebook page. What he found was that his cousin was great at the website stuff but had never heard of Google Local Services Ads or review management specifically for field service. I just filled the gap his cousin didn't cover. Would it make sense to have a quick call with your nephew so he can tell you if what I do overlaps with what he does?"

**ABC Response:**
"That's a good thing — having someone you trust looking after your marketing matters. (Acknowledge) I'm not here to replace what he does. What I specialize in is the review automation and Google Maps ranking specifically — it's usually a gap that website guys and social media managers don't cover because it lives in a different part of the Google ecosystem. (Bridge) Would it help to have a 15-minute call with your nephew so he can evaluate if what I do is something he's already handling? If he is, I'll bow out. (Close)"

**Follow-up question:** "What specifically is your nephew handling right now — website, social media, Google Ads? I want to make sure I'm not duplicating something he's already doing."

**When to walk away:** If the nephew is actively doing what you do and doing it well, there's no opening. If he's doing web and social but not review management or Google Maps optimization, you have a clear lane.

### Objection 18: "I tried an agency before and got burned — paid them $2,000 a month and got nothing"

**What it really means:** Past trauma specific to home services marketing agencies, which have a notoriously poor reputation in the trades. This is the home services version of Objection 7 and deserves its own handling.

**FFR Response:**
"I hear that — and honestly, the agency world has a serious problem when it comes to home services. Most agencies sell you a retainer, outsource your stuff overseas, send you a PDF report full of metrics that don't mean anything, and collect the check. A roofer I work with paid $2,400/month for 14 months, got a prettier website and zero more calls. What he found was that the difference was specificity — agencies that work with restaurants, law firms, AND roofers don't actually know any of those businesses. Everything I do is specific to field service businesses. And you can see your results in Google calls, not in a PDF of impressions."

**ABC Response:**
"I'd be surprised if you HADN'T been burned — most home service owners I talk to have a horror story about a marketing agency. (Acknowledge) Can I ask what specifically they were supposed to deliver and what you actually got? (Bridge) I want to know if what went wrong is the same thing I do. If it is, I'll tell you straight and walk away. If it's not, I might be able to show you a completely different experience. (Close)"

**Proof point:** Offer to show them a live Google dashboard for a current client in their trade — actual calls tracked, not impressions or clicks. Real numbers they recognize.

**When to walk away:** If they swore off all marketing vendors after a painful experience and have rebuilt entirely on referrals and direct work, the trust deficit may be too large. Plant a seed: "If you ever decide to try again, I'd want to be the one to earn your trust back. Here's my cell."

## Pricing Objections — Home Services Edition

### Tactic 1: Reframe Against Angi Spend

"Let's look at this differently. You're paying [Angi spend] per month for leads that go to 3 other contractors too. You're paying me [your price] per month for a system that makes YOUR phone ring with leads nobody else gets. The Angi leads you close 1 in 5. The Google inbound calls you close 1 in 2 — they called YOU specifically. That's what you're buying."

### Tactic 2: Cost Per Booked Job Comparison

"What's your average ticket? [X]. How many jobs does Google send you per month right now? [Y]. What I do for most companies is add 8-15 Google calls per month. At your close rate, that's [Z] more jobs per month, at [X] average ticket. That's [revenue] in additional monthly revenue from an investment of [your price]. Payback is in [timeframe]. Does that math change how you're looking at this?"

### Tactic 3: Cost of No Action (seasonal framing)

"Here's the real question: what does slow season cost you? You told me January is brutal. Last January, how many days did you have trucks sitting? At [X revenue per truck per day], that was [Y] in lost revenue. I'm not saying I can fix all of that — but if your Google presence is pulling in 10 more calls a month starting in October, how many of those turn into January revenue?"

### Tactic 4: Start Small / Pilot

"If the full price feels like too much of a commitment right now, here's what I'd suggest: start with just the review automation for [lower price]. No long-term contract, cancel anytime. If you don't see 10 new reviews in 60 days, I'll refund the first month. The risk is on me."

### Tactic 5: Monthly vs. Annual (for home services)

"I'd rather do monthly billing for you — I know home service cash flow isn't linear, and I don't want you paying for the full year upfront and then resenting it in January when it's slow. Month-to-month keeps both of us honest. You stay because it's working, not because you're locked in."

## Objection Prevention — Home Services

### 1. Lead with their Google profile, not your product

Before any pitch, look up their Google Business Profile. Open with: "I looked at your profile — you're at [rating] with [X] reviews. Your top competitor in the same zip code is at [4.8] with [180] reviews. That gap is costing you calls every week." — You've addressed the ROI objection before they can raise it.

### 2. Name-drop a local contractor in their trade

"I work with Tony who runs a plumbing company about 20 minutes from you." Peer credibility in home services is the single most powerful trust signal. Use it in your first sentence.

### 3. Acknowledge you know they get sold to constantly

"I know you get called by people selling marketing stuff every week, so I'm going to skip the pitch and just show you one thing." — Disarms the "not interested" objection before it forms.

### 4. Give value before asking for anything

Send their Google profile analysis, competitive comparison, or review gap report BEFORE the first call. Show up with insight, not a deck.

### 5. Respect the season

Never lead with growth-tool pitches during peak season. They can't hear you. Call in shoulder season, lead with: "Before your next peak season hits, I want to make sure you're set up to capture everything it brings."
